HalluTrace · project scope · senior project

Where do hallucinations actually come from?

Twenty-nine papers surveyed, six cut as out of scope, and one gap none of the rest fills. This page follows that line — what we found, the problem it exposes, our solution, what we use from other work against what we built ourselves, and how the nine weeks to the 31 October workshop deadline are spent.

03What we found

01

Not one of the twenty-nine opens the corpus

The near-misses are the instructive part. HalluLens defines extrinsic hallucination as inconsistency with training data and then declines to look — "given the vast and often opaque nature of training data, we chose Wikipedia data in our benchmark." That is a paper from the organization that trained Llama, treating its own corpus as unopenable. HaluEval states the limitation outright: it "does not investigate the underlying reasons behind the appearance of hallucinations."

02

These are not twenty-nine independent attempts — they are roughly five lineages

HaluEval seeds both HaluEval 2.0 and the HaluBench dataset behind Lynx. RAGTruth feeds both Lynx and FaithJudge. FaithBench feeds FaithJudge. FACTS Grounding expands into the FACTS Leaderboard. HalluBench, the most recent entry in the table, is five splits of TruthfulQA, SciFact and JudgeBench with a Turkish translation added. The Vectara leaderboard evolves in place. If none of the roots inspects pretraining data, every descendant inherits the blind spot by construction — what we document is a small number of foundational decisions propagated forward, not twenty-nine separate oversights.

03

The one paper that asked why has no descendants

HALoGEN is the only benchmark whose categories are defined by their relationship to pretraining data — Truth when the correct fact is present and the model still failed, Error when the corpus itself carries the wrong version, Silence when nothing is there at all. HALoGEN calls these Type A, B and C; we rename them for readability and keep the mapping explicit. In the derivation graph it sits as an isolated node: it descends from none of the surveyed benchmarks and none descends from it. Over a year on, nobody has built on it.

04The problem

A hallucination can be attacked at exactly three points, and those three differ entirely in cost, in tractability, and in who owns them.

Where you interveneWhat you doOwned by
The corpusRemove or correct the offending documentsWhoever builds the dataset
TrainingIncrease exposure, upweight rare facts, change the objectiveWhoever trains the model
InferenceRetrieval, abstention, decoding strategyWhoever deploys

The remedies are incompatible. If an error came from a bad document, cleaning the corpus fixes it and retrieval cannot — the datastore carries the same mistake. If the fact was present and recall failed, retrieval is exactly the right lever. If nothing was there at all, the only remedy is teaching the model to decline.

The goal

Produce one number that does not currently exist: what fraction of a model's hallucinations could be eliminated by fixing its training data, versus needing intervention during training, or at inference.

Three different teams with three different budgets are all allocating effort against the same population of errors, and none of them knows the split. Everything else in this project is machinery for producing that number.

On the objection that this is tool-driven

A fair challenge: did we pick corpus search because it was available, then invent a question to fit? Ask what fraction of hallucination is fixable in the corpus and open-data models stop being a convenience — they become the only instrument that can answer. The question forces the tool, not the other way around.

05Our solution

Label every hallucination twice — once for the kind of failure it is, once for where it came from in the pretraining data — then cross the two labels. Critically, the two labels are decided from different evidence, which is what makes crossing them meaningful rather than circular.

Axis 1 · from the output alone

What kind of failure

  • EXISTENCE — cites something that does not exist
  • ATTRIBUTION — the source is real but does not support the claim
  • NON-ABSTENTION — answers a question that has no correct answer

Checked against PyPI, Semantic Scholar, and a gazetteer. No human judgement needed.

Axis 2 · from the corpus — HALoGEN's Type A/B/C, renamed

Where it came from

  • CORRECT_IN_DATA — the fact was there; recall failed → training or inference
  • WRONG_IN_DATA — the error is genuinely in the corpus → the corpus
  • NOT_IN_DATA — nothing was there → abstention only

Two queries per claim into infini-gram; which one returns hits decides the label.

Which models, and why exactly these

The two axes have different entry requirements, and that single fact decides the whole study. Axis 1 needs only the model's output, so it runs on anything we can call. Axis 2 needs to read the corpus the model was trained on, and that corpus must also be searchable at the scale of thousands of individual lookups — which in practice means it has an infini-gram index.

The scope decision this study rests on

We study every model whose pretraining corpus is published and searchable. There are five of them, so we take all five rather than sampling.

This is not a benchmark and does not try to be one. A benchmark ranks models; this measures where their errors come from, and that can only be done where the training data can be opened. Because the set is small enough to take whole, the result describes a complete population rather than a sample — the question "why these five?" has the answer "because there are five".

The five, matched at roughly 7B

All five are base checkpoints, all within a few hundred million parameters of each other, and each was trained by a different group on a corpus built on a different principle. Using base models throughout removes the post-training confound entirely: nothing in the comparison varies because one model had SFT and another did not.

Two repo names that must be exactly right

OLMo 1.7 is now called OLMo-7B-0424, and the -hf suffix is not optional: the repo without it needs AI2's own olmo library rather than transformers.

Pythia must be pythia-6.9b, never pythia-6.9b-deduped. The index is Pile-train, the non-deduplicated corpus. Picking the deduped checkpoint pairs a model with a corpus it was not trained on, and nothing in the pipeline would warn us — the counts would come back looking perfectly reasonable and every provenance label from that model would be quietly wrong.

DCLM-7B may not serve on vLLM — settle this in gate week

apple/DCLM-7B is an OpenLM architecture, not Llama or GPT-NeoX. Its model card directs users to install open_lm and says nothing about vLLM. The plan assumes one vLLM instance serves all five, so this is the one model that could break that assumption.

Test vllm serve apple/DCLM-7B in week one, not in late September. If it fails there are three routes, in order: serve this one model through open_lm separately and accept the lower throughput; find a community conversion to HF format; or drop DCLM and run four corpora, which still clears the bar set above. The other four are architectures vLLM has supported for years.

What base-only costs, and how it is handled

Base models do not abstain on their own, and abstention is one of our three failure modes. Every prompt is therefore issued few-shot, with demonstrations that include a refusal, so the model has a template for declining available to it. Parse-failure rate is reported openly per model rather than quietly dropped, because a tolerant parser applied to a base model is exactly where silent selection creeps into results.

Having an index is not the same as being covered by it

All five corpora are indexed, but two of the five models were trained on more than the corpus their index covers. Both were trained in stages, and only the first stage is public as an index. This is not a rounding error we can wave through, because the missing slice is not random — it is the part our tasks ask about.

The bias has a direction, which is what makes it survivable

A fact sitting in the uncovered slice is counted as absent. Incomplete coverage therefore over-reports NOT_IN_DATA and under-reports CORRECT_IN_DATA — never the reverse.

Because the direction is known, NOT_IN_DATA is reported per model as an upper bound with its coverage fraction attached, rather than as a point estimate that quietly depends on how completely each index was built. Pythia, at full coverage, anchors the panel: if its NOT_IN_DATA share sits below the others, coverage is a candidate explanation and we say so. One specific rule follows — no strong NOT_IN_DATA claim is made from DCLM-7B on the code task while StarCoder is unindexed, because that is exactly where the answers would be.

Two ways to close these gaps, both cheap

OLMo 2 costs nothing but an email. AI2's own OlmoTrace configuration lists an index named v4-olmo-2-0325-32b-anneal-adapt — the annealing data is already indexed, just not exposed publicly. The request is to switch something on, not to build it.

DCLM we can close ourselves. StarCoder and ProofPile2 together are on the order of a terabyte — not the eight or twenty-five of a full pretraining corpus — so a single streaming pass with the same pattern set takes a few hours and a few dollars, and its counts add to the ones the API returns. This is where the one-pass procedure below stops being a contingency and becomes something we are likely to run.

Why corpora rather than sizes

An earlier version of this plan varied parameter count within one model family and held the corpus fixed. That answers a real question — how much of hallucination scaling repairs — but it is not this project's question. We want to know what hallucination comes from, and we want differences between models rather than between versions of one model. So the axis is inverted: hold scale roughly fixed, vary the training data.

The headline quantity

Corpus spread — how far the provenance mix moves when the training data changes and the scale does not.

Wide spread says the corpus is where the leverage is, and names which corpus does better. Narrow spread says something more surprising: that the provenance mix is stable across five independently built datasets, and is therefore a property of the task rather than of any one group's data decisions. Both outcomes are reportable, which is what makes this worth running. A study that only works if the numbers land one way is a bet, not an experiment.

The claim we will not make

These five models differ in more than their corpora. Architecture, tokenizer, token budget, training recipe and release year all move at once, so a difference between them cannot be attributed to the corpus alone, and we do not write that it can. What we report is between-model variation in the provenance mix, with the corpus named as one candidate driver among several.

The per-claim label is untouched by this. Whether a given fact appears in a given model's training data is a matter of fact, settled by search. The confound lives in the comparison across models, not in the measurement of any one of them — and saying precisely where it lives is the difference between a limitation and a flaw.

Why identical criteria matter more than they look

Change anything for these two and the empty column stops meaning "we cannot explain this" and starts meaning "we measured something else".

The row does two jobs. These two rows are therefore reported as end-to-end system behaviour, never as evidence about training data — and a refusal on policy grounds is counted in its own bucket rather than as an abstention.

Publishing a corpus is not the same as making it searchable. Several models release their pretraining data in full and still cannot be studied this way, because nobody has built an index over it and building one is far out of reach — roughly 7 bytes per token, which puts a 6T-token corpus at about 41 TB before temporary files.

The method itself is not the limit — a fixed query set can be answered by one streaming pass over any of these corpora, as set out below — but the bandwidth and the calendar are, and we would rather run five corpora properly than six in a hurry.

It also sharpens section 03's point. Even a fully open model can be unexplainable in practice, because openness of data and searchability of data are different properties and only the second one lets anyone ask where an error came from. The gap the survey documents is not only about labs that withhold data.

The lookup problem we have to solve first

Half our query pairs differ by a suffix — read_json against read_jsonl, a paper title with one word changed, a year with one digit changed. Any substring count will find the shorter string inside the longer one and report a hit that is not evidence. This is not a quirk of one tool; it is inherent to substring counting, and infini-gram counts token n-grams, so it is exposed too.

One of the three query forms per fact is therefore boundary-anchored — a trailing delimiter is included, so pandas.read_json( cannot match inside pandas.read_jsonl( — and a sample of matches is pulled with the find endpoint and read by hand. A prefix-pair test case goes into the gate-week script, so that if the counts do collide we know in week one rather than at analysis.

If there is no index — how the method scales

This procedure does three jobs. It closes the DCLM coverage gap by scanning StarCoder and ProofPile2, which is small enough to be practical and is the one place we expect to actually run it. It answers the obvious question about the excluded table above — could you not just do it yourselves? And it is the fallback if the public API becomes unavailable mid-project.

Building an index is not the answer. At roughly 7 bytes per token plus temporary files, a 6T-token corpus needs about 41 TB before anything is downloaded, and infini-gram mini reports 99 days on a 128-vCPU node to index 83 TB. But an index exists to answer queries nobody has thought of yet, and ours are all known before we start. Roughly 12,000 fixed strings — every claim, in three query forms, in a correct and an incorrect variant — can be compiled into one Aho-Corasick automaton and counted in a single streaming pass, at a cost that does not grow with the number of patterns.

CompileAll ~12,000 patterns into one automaton — a trie with failure links, so the read head never backs up. About 230 MB of memory.
NormaliseNFKC, smart quotes and dashes folded, whitespace collapsed, lowercased — applied identically to patterns and corpus. This rule is the definition of "appears in the corpus" and belongs in the paper.
StreamDocuments pulled with datasets streaming, matched, discarded. Nothing is stored, so the disk cost is zero.
FilterEvery match carries its position, so a hit is kept only when the characters on either side are not word characters.
EmitPer-pattern counts plus a window of context around the first few matches, for hand inspection.
Why not simply use ripgrep, which is four times faster

Because it gets the answer wrong on exactly our query shape. Given the pair pandas.read_json / pandas.read_jsonl over text containing both, a single ripgrep pass reported the correct form 3 times and the hallucinated form zero times — a regex engine consumes each position once, so the shorter pattern swallows the longer one. That flips the verdict from MIXED to CORRECT_IN_DATA: the paper would say the corpus was right and the model misremembered, when in fact the error is sitting in the training data. Sorting patterns longest-first does not fix it; it moves the error to the other count.

Aho-Corasick is built to report overlapping matches, so it returns 3 and 1, and the boundary filter then removes the one occurrence that was only a substring of the longer string — leaving 2 and 1, which is the truth. The failure is silent: no crash, no warning, just provenance labels that are wrong in a consistent direction. A prefix-pair test case is therefore a permanent fixture of the test suite, run before every scan.

Measured on this query size: 15 MB/s per core through the automaton against 68 MB/s for ripgrep. Sixteen cores of the slower, correct method still outruns a 1 Gbps connection by a factor of two, so the bottleneck is bandwidth, not matching — an 8 TB corpus is roughly eighteen hours of streaming and about $8 of CPU time, with no GPU and no storage. Optimising the part that is not the bottleneck, at the price of correctness, would be a bad trade.

Taken from prior work — cited openly

What the field already had

  • Prompt sets — HALoGEN, permissively licensed
  • Reference vs content grounding — HalluHard
  • Non-abstention as a failure mode — HalluLens, AbstentionBench
  • infini-gram as the corpus search tool
  • The idea that outputs can be traced to training data — OLMoTrace
  • Models and corpora from AI2 and EleutherAI

Every item here was built to be reused, under published licences. Reusing prompts is a control, not a shortcut: change the questions and the criteria at once, and no difference in the results can be attributed to either. Holding the prompts fixed is what lets our numbers sit beside HALoGEN's and mean anything.

Ours

What nobody has done

  • Crossing the two axes — absent from all twenty-nine
  • One decision rule across every task, so results compare
  • The query protocol — how many forms, how combined, how bounds are reported
  • Automatic labeling benchmark-wide, not spot-checks
  • Retaining raw counts → the frequency curve and the Kalai singleton test
  • An error bar on proxy-corpus attribution — nobody has measured this
  • Complete coverage of the searchable population — all five, not a sample
  • The excluded-models table — open data and unsearchable data are different things, and nobody has written that down
  • The twenty-nine-paper survey, verified against primary sources
  • The intervention-point framing — A/B/C as an address, not an explanation
If someone says this is just combining other people's work

This project does not propose a method. It takes a measurement.

HALoGEN established that three causes exist. We establish how much of each there is — moving the claim from existence to magnitude, which is a different kind of scientific statement, not a recombination. Biology does not call an epidemiological survey "just a shovel plus a lab."

And the test that settles it: an assembly cannot produce a wrong answer — a measurement can. Ours can be wrong in several ways. The Kalai prediction may fail. Error may come back at two percent. OLMo may come back at two percent. The five corpora may show no spread at all. That the result can be wrong is what proves it is a finding rather than a construction.

The nearest neighbour, and how we differ from it

OLMoTrace (AI2, ACL 2025 Demo, arXiv:2504.07096) already traces model output back to training data in real time, on infini-gram, and ships inside the Ai2 Playground. It is the closest thing to this project that exists, it comes from the lab that made both the models and the index, and anyone reading our paper will know it. We cite it as the tool it is.

It matches verbatim spans, not facts. It has no notion of a correct form against an incorrect one, assigns no label, and aggregates nothing — it is a system paper whose own text lists hallucination as a use case that users can explore. OLMoTrace built the instrument and said what it could be pointed at. Nobody has pointed it at a whole benchmark and reported what came back. The distance between a demo and a measurement is the distance between that paper and this one.

08Abstract

Large language models hallucinate, and a substantial literature now measures how often. We surveyed twenty-nine hallucination benchmarks and verified each against its primary source: all of them quantify hallucination and none inspects the pretraining corpus to establish where an error came from. The one paper that proposes a provenance taxonomy applies it as a case study and has no descendants. As a result, the practical question — whether a given hallucination is addressable in the training data, during training, or only at inference — has no measured answer.

We present a benchmark that labels every hallucination twice. The first axis records the kind of failure (citing a source that does not exist, misdescribing one that does, or answering a question that has no answer) and is decided from the model output alone by lookup against authoritative indexes. The second axis records provenance (Truth, Error, or Silence) and is decided by searching the model's own pretraining corpus with infini-gram. Because the two axes are decided from disjoint evidence, crossing them is informative rather than circular.

We apply this to every model whose pretraining corpus is both published and searchable — five base checkpoints at roughly seven billion parameters, trained by four groups on five differently constructed corpora. Because that set is small enough to take whole, the result describes a complete population rather than a sample. Two closed commercial models are run on one task under identical criteria and reported separately, not to be evaluated but to mark the boundary of what can be explained at all. We report the resulting cross-tabulation, the spread of the provenance mix across the five corpora, the fraction of claims whose label depends on which corpus is searched, the disagreement introduced by substituting a proxy corpus, and the relationship between corpus frequency and hallucination rate. We release the cause-labeled corpus, with the raw corpus counts and the retrieved evidence attached to every judgment.

09Introduction

Language models are now routine tools for finding and synthesizing information, and they continue to produce fluent, confident claims that no evidence supports. The damage lies less in the errors than in their invisibility: a fabricated citation carries the same typographic authority as a real one, and a hallucinated package import runs until it does not — a failure already demonstrated as a software supply-chain attack surface. In both cases the surface form of the output gives the reader no signal that anything is wrong.

The community has responded with a dense wave of benchmarks. We surveyed twenty-nine, verifying each against its arXiv or ACL Anthology source rather than relying on secondary summaries, and recorded one field above all others: whether the paper opens the model's pretraining data to explain where an error originated. All twenty-nine answer no. The near-misses are instructive rather than contradictory — HalluLens defines extrinsic hallucination as inconsistency with training data and then declines to look, substituting Wikipedia because "the vast and often opaque nature of training data" makes the corpus impractical to inspect; HaluEval states outright that it does not investigate underlying reasons.

These are not twenty-nine independent efforts. Mapping their stated derivations yields roughly five lineages, and if none of the roots inspects pretraining data, every descendant inherits the blind spot by construction. The exception proves the point: HALoGEN, the only benchmark whose categories are defined by their relationship to pretraining data, sits in the derivation graph as an isolated node — descending from none of the surveyed benchmarks, and with none descending from it.

The gap matters because the remedies are incompatible. A hallucination can be attacked at three points — the pretraining corpus, the training process, or inference — and those three differ entirely in cost, in tractability, and in who owns them. If an error came from a bad document, cleaning the corpus removes it and retrieval augmentation cannot, because the datastore carries the same mistake. If the fact was present and recall failed, retrieval is exactly the right lever. If nothing was present at all, the only remedy is teaching the model to decline. Three different teams with three different budgets currently allocate effort against the same population of errors without knowing the split.

This paper measures that split. We contribute: (i) the first cross-tabulation of hallucination failure mode against training-data provenance, applied across a whole prompt set rather than spot-checked; (ii) a single decision rule that holds across three heterogeneous tasks, making their results comparable; (iii) complete coverage of the population of models whose pretraining corpus can actually be searched, together with a record of the open-data models that cannot be reached because publishing a corpus and indexing one are different things, together with the single-pass procedure by which the method extends to them; (iv) a released corpus of cause-labeled hallucinations with raw corpus counts and retrieved evidence attached; and (v) a verified survey of twenty-nine benchmarks along a dimension no prior work has compiled. We do not propose a benchmark and do not rank models. We are explicit about what this does not establish: corpus presence and absence show where an error is addressable in principle, not that a given intervention would succeed; because exact-string search cannot detect facts expressed in other surface forms, and because two of the five indexes cover only the first training stage of their model, NOT_IN_DATA is reported as an upper bound with its coverage fraction attached; and because closed endpoints may retrieve without disclosing it, results on that tier describe system behaviour rather than model behaviour and are never used to support a claim about pretraining data.

Measures

Let C be the set of atomic claims extracted from a model's responses, partitioned into supported claims C_sup, hallucinated claims C_hal, and claims the verifier could not decide C_unv. Let R be the set of responses, and let m index a model configuration — quantities written x(m) are computed per configuration and compared across them. The first five formulas score a single model; the four that follow are the comparisons the multi-model design exists to make.

Hallucination rate
H = |C_hal| / ( |C_hal| + |C_sup| )
Undecidable claims are excluded from the denominator rather than counted as either outcome. Reported per model and per task.
Abstention rate
A = |R_abstained| / |R|
Reported separately, never folded into H. Were abstentions placed in the denominator, a model that refuses everything would score best. Policy refusals are counted in their own bucket and excluded from R_abstained, so the two illustration models are reported beside the five subjects rather than pooled with them — a refusal on policy grounds and a decline from having no answer are the same visible event and opposite in meaning.
Parse-failure rate
F = |R_parse-fail| / |R|
Reported openly. Base models without instruction tuning fail to parse more often, and hiding that rate would make the remaining numbers look selected.
Verifier validation
against human annotation
P = TP / (TP + FP)
R = TP / (TP + FN)
F1 = 2PR / (P + R)
200 items per task, two annotators each, with the automatic verdict as the prediction and the human consensus as ground truth. Acceptance threshold: agreement ≥ 0.85. The count doubled when the model list was cut to ten — the freed effort went here rather than into more models.
Inter-annotator agreement
κ = (p_o − p_e) / (1 − p_e)
Cohen's κ, where p_o is observed agreement and p_e the agreement expected by chance. Reported alongside raw agreement, since raw agreement alone is inflated when one class dominates.
Corpus counts
c_true(i) = max over query forms q of count(q, correct_i)
c_false(i) = max over query forms q of count(q, halluc_i)
Three query forms per fact: literal, function words stripped, entity order swapped. The maximum is taken because any single hit proves presence; every individual count is stored, not just the maximum.
Provenance label
CORRECT_IN_DATA if c_true > 0 and c_false = 0
WRONG_IN_DATA if c_true = 0 and c_false > 0
NOT_IN_DATA if c_true = 0 and c_false = 0
MIXED if c_true > 0 and c_false > 0
Applied identically across all three tasks. N/A where no unique correct answer exists, which is distinct from NOT_IN_DATA and must not be merged with it. Where the index covers only part of what a model was trained on, a fact in the uncovered slice reads as absent, so NOT_IN_DATA is an upper bound and is reported with the coverage fraction beside it — 98.7% for OLMo 2, 93% for DCLM-7B before the StarCoder scan, complete for Pythia.
Cross-tabulation cell
P(cause = k | mode = j) = n_jk / n_j
The principal result. n_jk is the count of hallucinations with failure mode j and provenance k; n_j is the total for that mode. Reported with Wilson confidence intervals over the five samples.
Provenance composition
π_k(m) = n_k(m) / |C_hal(m)|
for k ∈ {CORRECT_IN_DATA, WRONG_IN_DATA, NOT_IN_DATA, MIXED}
The share of model m's hallucinations carrying each provenance label. Composition, not raw counts — otherwise a model that simply hallucinates less would be mistaken for one whose hallucinations have a different cause.
Corpus spread
scale approximately fixed
S_k = max over m of π_k(m) − min over m of π_k(m)
over the five open-data models
How far the share of provenance class k moves when the training data changes and the parameter count does not. Claimed as non-zero only where the extreme models' Wilson intervals do not overlap. S_NOT_IN_DATA is the headline — a wide spread says the corpus shapes what a model cannot know, a narrow one says the provenance mix is stable across independently built datasets.
Label disagreement
the same claim, five corpora
D = |{ i : label(i, m) not constant over m }| / |C_hal ∪ C_sup|
The fraction of claims whose provenance label depends on which model's corpus is searched. D near zero would mean the search is detecting the open web rather than each model's own data — a check on the method, not on the models.
Singleton rate
Kalai et al. test
s = |{ i : c_true(i) = 1 }| / |C_hal ∪ C_sup|

prediction: H ≥ s
The theory predicts hallucination rate is lower-bounded by the fraction of facts appearing exactly once. Reported alongside H per frequency bucket (0, 1, 2–10, 11–100, 100+). Because we count string occurrences rather than facts, s is an upper bound.
Proxy-corpus agreement
A_proxy = |{ i : label_proxy(i) = label_true(i) }| / N
Labels derived from RedPajama or C4 compared against labels from the model's actual corpus. Quantifies the error incurred by prior work that substitutes a proxy corpus for an unavailable one.
