What we borrowed, what we built

The scope in brief, what comes from prior work and what is ours, and short answers to the questions this project keeps being asked. Open this one when someone challenges the work; the full detail lives in the plan.

01Scope

The question: across which intervention points — the pretraining corpus, the training process, or inference — do an open model's hallucinations fall, and in what proportion?

What we do

We take three tasks where hallucination can be verified automatically, run seven models over them, and label every hallucinated claim twice — once for the kind of failure it is, once for where it came from in the pretraining data. Crossing those two labels produces a table showing which kinds of error arise from which kinds of cause, which is the result the field currently lacks.

How we do it

The failure-mode label comes from the output alone: a cited paper either exists in Semantic Scholar or it does not, a package either exists on PyPI or it does not, a question with no correct answer was either refused or it was not. The provenance label comes from the corpus. For each hallucinated claim we build two search queries — the string the model produced, and the string of the correct answer — and count both against the model's actual pretraining data using infini-gram. Which of the two returns hits determines whether the fact was available and misrecalled, present but wrong, or absent altogether. Because the two labels are decided from different evidence, crossing them is meaningful rather than circular.

Why it matters

The three causes demand incompatible remedies, and they belong to different people. If the error came from a bad document, cleaning the corpus fixes it and retrieval cannot — the datastore carries the same mistake. If the fact was present and the model failed to recall it, retrieval is exactly the right lever. If nothing was there at all, the only remedy is teaching the model to decline. Corpus, training and deployment are three different teams with three different budgets, and today all three allocate effort against the same population of errors without knowing the split.

Why us

Answering it requires searching the data a model was actually trained on, which is possible for only a handful of model families. That constraint is what has kept the question open — and it is why the study is built around OLMo 2 and Pythia rather than the models most people use.

02Borrowed and built

Borrowed — cited openly, every item

What the field already had

  • The Type A/B/C concept — HALoGEN
  • Prompt sets — HALoGEN, permissively licensed
  • Reference vs content grounding — HalluHard
  • Non-abstention as a failure mode — HalluLens, AbstentionBench
  • infini-gram as the corpus search tool
  • Models and corpora from AI2 and EleutherAI

Every item here was built to be reused. Using it follows the field's norms rather than cutting a corner.

Built

What nobody has done

  • Crossing the two axes — absent from all 29 surveyed papers
  • One decision rule across every task, so results compare
  • The query protocol — how many forms, how combined, how bounds are reported
  • Automatic labeling benchmark-wide, not spot-checks
  • Retaining raw counts → frequency curve and the Kalai test
  • An error bar on proxy-corpus attribution
  • A 29-paper survey verified against primary sources
  • The intervention-locus framing — A/B/C as an address, not an explanation

03Questions this keeps getting

On the problem

What is the problem, precisely?

Hallucination can be attacked at three points — the corpus, the training process, or inference — and those three differ entirely in cost and in who owns them. Nobody knows how a real population of errors distributes across them, so all three teams currently allocate effort blind.

"Root cause" of what, exactly?

Not cause in general. In engineering a root cause is what, once changed, stops the failure recurring — so the meaningful question is where would you have to intervene. Type A/B/C is not an explanation of an error; it is an address for it.

Isn't this tool-driven? You had corpus search and invented a question to fit it.

Ask what fraction of hallucination is fixable in the corpus, and open-data models stop being a convenience — they become the only instrument that can answer. The question forces the tool, not the other way around.

On novelty

How is this different from HALoGEN?

HALoGEN proposed the concept; we build the instrument. It established the three causes exist by spot-checking dozens of cases across a few scenarios, using a different method in each, mostly against proxy corpora. We make it one decision rule labeling every instance against real corpora — then cross it with the kind of failure, which HALoGEN never does.

And from the other twenty-eight?

Four properties are needed together: it studies hallucination, inspects the real corpus, labels per instance, and covers a whole benchmark. Every prior work has some. None has all four.

Why not design your own taxonomy?

The field already has seven mutually incompatible ones — our own survey documents them. An eighth makes things worse. We took the one that answers the question nobody else asks and made it measurable.

Isn't reusing their prompts a shortcut?

It makes our numbers directly comparable to HALoGEN's, which is a methodological virtue. They published the licences precisely so the material would be reused. Effort belongs where nothing exists yet.

So this is just combining other people's work?

Yes — and that is why it has not been done. Assembling the pieces needs an open-data model, one rule that holds across every task, and the discipline to store raw counts from day one. A year after HALoGEN, nobody has assembled them, and HALoGEN itself has no descendants.

On limits

Can you run this on GPT, Claude or Gemini?

We can measure them — axis 1 needs only the output. We cannot explain them, because there is no corpus to search. They appear in the failure-mode results and never in the cross-tabulation.

So it only works on open-source models?

Open data, which is narrower than open source. Llama, Qwen and Mistral release weights but not their corpora, so they are measured, not explained. Only OLMo 2 and Pythia release both.

Does this reduce hallucination?

No. It measures and explains; impact arrives when someone else acts on the numbers. "We produce the number this decision requires, and nobody currently has it" is both stronger and defensible.

What if the corpus-fixable share turns out to be tiny?

That is an equally valuable answer, and worth a great deal to anyone currently spending money on data cleaning. The interpretation rules are fixed in advance precisely so this outcome cannot be read as failure.

04Two positions to fall back on

If every other argument gets contested, retreat here. Neither needs a comparison to prior work, because neither exists in the literature at all.

02

An error bar on proxy-corpus attribution

Others substitute proxy corpora without knowing the cost, because nobody held the real corpus and a proxy at once. We hold both.

The line not to cross

Closed models may be measured on failure mode and reported as such, but they never enter the cross-tabulation. Their Type A/B/C columns would be guesses wearing the costume of measurements — and the empty cells make the point better than filled ones would.
