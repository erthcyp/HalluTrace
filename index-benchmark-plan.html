Where do hallucinations actually come from?

Twenty-nine papers surveyed, six cut as out of scope, and one gap none of the rest fills. This page follows that line — what we found, the problem it exposes, our solution, what we use from other work against what we built ourselves, and how the nine weeks to the 31 October workshop deadline are spent.

03What we found

01

Not one of the twenty-nine opens the corpus

The near-misses are the instructive part. HalluLens defines extrinsic hallucination as inconsistency with training data and then declines to look — "given the vast and often opaque nature of training data, we chose Wikipedia data in our benchmark." That is a paper from the organization that trained Llama, treating its own corpus as unopenable. HaluEval states the limitation outright: it "does not investigate the underlying reasons behind the appearance of hallucinations."

02

These are not twenty-nine independent attempts — they are roughly five lineages

HaluEval seeds both HaluEval 2.0 and the HaluBench dataset behind Lynx. RAGTruth feeds both Lynx and FaithJudge. FaithBench feeds FaithJudge. FACTS Grounding expands into the FACTS Leaderboard. HalluBench, the most recent entry in the table, is five splits of TruthfulQA, SciFact and JudgeBench with a Turkish translation added. The Vectara leaderboard evolves in place. If none of the roots inspects pretraining data, every descendant inherits the blind spot by construction — what we document is a small number of foundational decisions propagated forward, not twenty-nine separate oversights.

03

The one paper that asked why has no descendants

HALoGEN is the only benchmark whose categories are defined by their relationship to pretraining data — Truth when the correct fact is present and the model still failed, Error when the corpus itself carries the wrong version, Silence when nothing is there at all. HALoGEN calls these Type A, B and C; we rename them for readability and keep the mapping explicit. In the derivation graph it sits as an isolated node: it descends from none of the surveyed benchmarks and none descends from it. Over a year on, nobody has built on it.

04The problem

A hallucination can be attacked at exactly three points, and those three differ entirely in cost, in tractability, and in who owns them.

Where you interveneWhat you doOwned by
The corpusRemove or correct the offending documentsWhoever builds the dataset
TrainingIncrease exposure, upweight rare facts, change the objectiveWhoever trains the model
InferenceRetrieval, abstention, decoding strategyWhoever deploys

The remedies are incompatible. If an error came from a bad document, cleaning the corpus fixes it and retrieval cannot — the datastore carries the same mistake. If the fact was present and recall failed, retrieval is exactly the right lever. If nothing was there at all, the only remedy is teaching the model to decline.

The goal

Produce one number that does not currently exist: what fraction of a model's hallucinations could be eliminated by fixing its training data, versus needing intervention during training, or at inference.

Three different teams with three different budgets are all allocating effort against the same population of errors, and none of them knows the split. Everything else in this project is machinery for producing that number.

On the objection that this is tool-driven

A fair challenge: did we pick corpus search because it was available, then invent a question to fit? Ask what fraction of hallucination is fixable in the corpus and open-data models stop being a convenience — they become the only instrument that can answer. The question forces the tool, not the other way around.

05Our solution

Label every hallucination twice — once for the kind of failure it is, once for where it came from in the pretraining data — then cross the two labels. Critically, the two labels are decided from different evidence, which is what makes crossing them meaningful rather than circular.

Axis 1 · from the output alone

What kind of failure

  • EXISTENCE — cites something that does not exist
  • ATTRIBUTION — the source is real but does not support the claim
  • NON-ABSTENTION — answers a question that has no correct answer

Checked against PyPI, Semantic Scholar, and a gazetteer. No human judgement needed.

Axis 2 · from the corpus — HALoGEN's Type A/B/C, renamed

Where it came from

  • TRUTH — the fact was there; recall failed → training or inference
  • ERROR — the error is genuinely in the corpus → the corpus
  • SILENCE — nothing was there → abstention only

Two queries per claim into infini-gram; which one returns hits decides the label.

Which models we can use

The two axes have different entry requirements, and that single fact decides the whole model list. Axis 1 needs only the model's output, so it runs on anything we can call. Axis 2 needs to read the corpus the model was trained on — and that corpus must also be searchable, which in practice means it has an infini-gram index.

So the models split into two groups, not by choice but by what each one permits. The asymmetry in cost between them is what shapes the plan: adding a group 2 model is nearly free, while adding a group 1 model buys another round of attribution work.

Group 1 · Axis 1 — every model we can call

Axis 1 needs nothing but the output, so it runs on all of them — the open-data models included, and the closed frontier models too. Adding one costs a generation run and automatic labeling, so we take the current field broadly rather than sampling it. Three tiers, listed by how much of the model is visible.

Why B and C are separate rows when both measure the same

Tiers B and C are indistinguishable in the data. They are separated because they are evidence for different things.

Neither publishes a corpus, so both carry n/a in every cause column and both are scored on axis 1 by exactly the same procedure. Collapsing them would lose nothing numerically and cost the argument one of its two halves. Tier B shows that the corpus gap survives maximal good faith — these labs released weights, code and in several cases the data recipe, and still did not release the data, so the gap is structural rather than a matter of commercial secrecy. Tier C shows that the gap covers the models the field actually ranks; without it a reviewer can dismiss the finding as a fact about research models. One tier answers why does no one do this, the other answers does it matter.

The sentence for the paper

Tier B is not yet attributable. Tier C cannot be made attributable by anyone outside the provider.

That difference is invisible in the table's cells and decisive for what the table means. It also states the project's larger point in one line: whether a hallucination can be explained is a property of how a model was released, not of how good the model is. The three tiers are a ladder of disclosure, and the ladder is itself a result — read down the Axis 2? column and the field's blind spot has a shape.

What we cannot rule out on tier C — stated as a limitation, not discovered later

A closed endpoint may retrieve. If a provider runs search or RAG behind the API, what we measure is no longer hallucination from parametric memory — and parametric memory is the only thing a corpus-provenance study is about. We cannot verify the absence of retrieval, so tier C results are reported as end-to-end system behaviour rather than model behaviour, and are never used to support a claim about pretraining data.

A refusal is also not an abstention. A closed model declining on policy grounds and a base model declining because it holds no answer produce the same visible event and mean opposite things. Tier C abstention rates are therefore reported beside tiers A and B rather than pooled with them, and refusals carrying explicit policy language are counted in a separate bucket.

What tier C costs us, beyond money

The price of including it is reproducibility. API models change without notice behind a stable name, so the exact model version and run date are recorded with every result, and the thesis states plainly that this tier cannot be re-run the way tiers A and B can. Provider terms on publishing comparative evaluations are checked before the sweep, since several restrict it.

We accept that price because the empty cause column beside a frontier model states the project's problem more plainly than a paragraph could.

Renting by the hour changes how we work, not just what we pay

An idle rented GPU bills exactly like a busy one, so the pipeline is built and debugged locally against a 1B checkpoint, the full prompt set is prepared offline, and the GPU is rented only to run a prepared batch and released immediately after. The cost risk here is not the hourly rate but hours spent debugging on a running meter — the discipline is to never open a rented instance without a batch ready to run.

Why four corpora, rather than four sizes

An earlier version of this plan varied parameter count across one model family and held the corpus fixed. It answered a real question — how much of hallucination scaling can repair — but it is not the question this project is asking. We want to know what hallucination comes from, and we want differences between models rather than between versions of one model. So the axis is inverted: hold the scale roughly fixed and vary the training data.

The four tier A models sit within a few hundred million parameters of each other and were trained by four different labs on four corpora built on four different philosophies — accumulation (the Pile), open reproduction (RedPajama), aggressive filtering (DCLM), and curated mixing (OLMo-mix). Each publishes its data and each has an infini-gram index, so for every one of them the question was this fact in the data this model read has an answer.

What replaces the scale curve

Corpus spread: how far the provenance mix moves when the training data changes and the scale does not.

Wide spread says the corpus is where the leverage is, and names which corpus does better. Narrow spread says something more surprising — that the provenance mix is stable across independently built datasets, and therefore a property of the task rather than of any one lab's data decisions. Both outcomes are reportable, which is what makes this worth running. A study that only works if the numbers come out one way is a bet, not an experiment.

The claim we will not make

These four models differ in more than their corpora. Architecture, tokenizer, token budget, training recipe and release year all move at the same time, so a difference between them cannot be attributed to the corpus alone, and we do not write that it can. What we report is between-model variation in the provenance mix, with the corpus named as one candidate driver among several.

The per-claim label is not affected by any of this. Whether a given fact appears in a given model's training data is a matter of fact, settled by search, and it stays exact no matter how the models differ. The confound lives in the comparison across models, not in the measurement of any one of them — and saying exactly where it lives is the difference between a limitation and a flaw.

The rule that keeps this affordable

Go wide on group 1. Go deep, in a fixed order, on group 2.

A group 1 model costs one generation run and automatic labeling — so breadth there is close to free, and it turns "does this generalize?" from a hope into a measurement across eight models. A group 2 model costs all of that plus attribution, sampling and manual inspection. But that extra cost is paid per corpus, not per model: a corpus query is keyed on the pair (claim, corpus), so every OLMo 2 size hits the same cache and the second, third and fourth sizes pay only for generation and for claims not seen before. Adding a model on a different corpus is what buys a fresh query set. Attribution effort therefore Each tier A model now opens a corpus of its own, so unlike the earlier plan there is no shared cache to lean on — four models means four query sets. That is the price of asking a between-model question, and it is why tier A stops at four. Attribution order is fixed by how much each one adds: OLMo 2 7B first as the reference, then DCLM for the sharpest contrast in philosophy, then Pythia and RedPajama-INCITE.

For the models outside group 2, the cause columns stay empty, marked n/a — corpus not public rather than left blank. Blank reads as unfinished; n/a reads as impossible. Those empty cells make the project's point better than invented numbers would.

One risk closed early

infini-gram indexes olmo-mix-1124 — the exact pretraining mix behind the OLMo 2 checkpoints, not a stand-in. We search the corpus the model actually read, so a count of zero means the fact is absent rather than that we queried the wrong collection. The same holds for every group 2 model: each is paired with the index of the corpus it actually read, never a stand-in.

Every item here was built to be reused, under published licences. Reusing prompts is a control, not a shortcut: change the questions and the criteria at once, and no difference in the results can be attributed to either. Holding the prompts fixed is what lets our numbers sit beside HALoGEN's and mean anything.

Ours

What nobody has done

  • Crossing the two axes — absent from all twenty-nine
  • One decision rule across every task, so results compare
  • The query protocol — how many forms, how combined, how bounds are reported
  • Automatic labeling benchmark-wide, not spot-checks
  • Retaining raw counts → the frequency curve and the Kalai singleton test
  • An error bar on proxy-corpus attribution — nobody has measured this
  • Near-complete coverage of open-data models — the set is small enough to take whole rather than sample
  • The twenty-nine-paper survey, verified against primary sources
  • The intervention-point framing — A/B/C as an address, not an explanation
If someone says this is just combining other people's work

This project does not propose a method. It takes a measurement.

HALoGEN established that three causes exist. We establish how much of each there is — moving the claim from existence to magnitude, which is a different kind of scientific statement, not a recombination. Biology does not call an epidemiological survey "just a shovel plus a lab."

And the test that settles it: an assembly cannot produce a wrong answer — a measurement can. Ours can be wrong in several ways. The Kalai prediction may fail. Error may come back at two percent. OLMo may come back at two percent. The four corpora may show no spread at all. That the result can be wrong is what proves it is a finding rather than a construction.

08Abstract

Large language models hallucinate, and a substantial literature now measures how often. We surveyed twenty-nine hallucination benchmarks and verified each against its primary source: all of them quantify hallucination and none inspects the pretraining corpus to establish where an error came from. The one paper that proposes a provenance taxonomy applies it as a case study and has no descendants. As a result, the practical question — whether a given hallucination is addressable in the training data, during training, or only at inference — has no measured answer.

We present a benchmark that labels every hallucination twice. The first axis records the kind of failure (citing a source that does not exist, misdescribing one that does, or answering a question that has no answer) and is decided from the model output alone by lookup against authoritative indexes. The second axis records provenance (Truth, Error, or Silence) and is decided by searching the model's own pretraining corpus with infini-gram. Because the two axes are decided from disjoint evidence, crossing them is informative rather than circular.

We evaluate eight models on the first axis — open-data, open-weight and closed commercial alike — of which four, all publishing both weights and an indexed pretraining corpus, also receive provenance labels. Those four are matched at roughly seven billion parameters and were trained by four different labs on four differently constructed corpora, so the comparison between them holds scale approximately fixed while the training data varies. We report the resulting cross-tabulation, the spread of the provenance mix across the four corpora, the disagreement introduced by substituting a proxy corpus, and the relationship between corpus frequency and hallucination rate. We release the cause-labeled corpus, with the raw corpus counts and the retrieved evidence attached to every judgment.

09Introduction

Language models are now routine tools for finding and synthesizing information, and they continue to produce fluent, confident claims that no evidence supports. The damage lies less in the errors than in their invisibility: a fabricated citation carries the same typographic authority as a real one, and a hallucinated package import runs until it does not — a failure already demonstrated as a software supply-chain attack surface. In both cases the surface form of the output gives the reader no signal that anything is wrong.

The community has responded with a dense wave of benchmarks. We surveyed twenty-nine, verifying each against its arXiv or ACL Anthology source rather than relying on secondary summaries, and recorded one field above all others: whether the paper opens the model's pretraining data to explain where an error originated. All twenty-nine answer no. The near-misses are instructive rather than contradictory — HalluLens defines extrinsic hallucination as inconsistency with training data and then declines to look, substituting Wikipedia because "the vast and often opaque nature of training data" makes the corpus impractical to inspect; HaluEval states outright that it does not investigate underlying reasons.

These are not twenty-nine independent efforts. Mapping their stated derivations yields roughly five lineages, and if none of the roots inspects pretraining data, every descendant inherits the blind spot by construction. The exception proves the point: HALoGEN, the only benchmark whose categories are defined by their relationship to pretraining data, sits in the derivation graph as an isolated node — descending from none of the surveyed benchmarks, and with none descending from it.

The gap matters because the remedies are incompatible. A hallucination can be attacked at three points — the pretraining corpus, the training process, or inference — and those three differ entirely in cost, in tractability, and in who owns them. If an error came from a bad document, cleaning the corpus removes it and retrieval augmentation cannot, because the datastore carries the same mistake. If the fact was present and recall failed, retrieval is exactly the right lever. If nothing was present at all, the only remedy is teaching the model to decline. Three different teams with three different budgets currently allocate effort against the same population of errors without knowing the split.

This paper measures that split. We contribute: (i) the first cross-tabulation of hallucination failure mode against training-data provenance, applied across a whole benchmark rather than spot-checked; (ii) a single decision rule that holds across three heterogeneous tasks, making their results comparable; (iii) a released corpus of cause-labeled hallucinations with raw corpus counts and retrieved evidence attached; and (iv) a verified survey of twenty-nine benchmarks along a dimension no prior work has compiled. We are explicit about what this does not establish: corpus presence and absence show where an error is addressable in principle, not that a given intervention would succeed; because exact-string search cannot detect facts expressed in other surface forms, the fabrication rate is reported as an upper bound; and because closed endpoints may retrieve without disclosing it, results on that tier describe system behaviour rather than model behaviour and are never used to support a claim about pretraining data.

10Framework

The outer boundary is the machine. Everything drawn inside it — input, the process itself, and the output — runs on one rented cloud GPU under the stack named along the top edge and spelled out in full at the bottom. The specific card and provider are chosen at setup and deliberately left out of the figure; the software versions are pinned at gate week and recorded in the lockfile. External services sit outside that boundary because they run on somebody else's hardware: we call them, we do not operate them, and if one of them changes or goes down there is nothing we can do but say so in the paper. Dashed arrows cross the boundary at exactly the three points where the pipeline reaches out.

One prompt enters at START, a doubly-labeled claim leaves at Aggregate, and the diamond is the only place the model tiers diverge.

Measures

Let C be the set of atomic claims extracted from a model's responses, partitioned into supported claims C_sup, hallucinated claims C_hal, and claims the verifier could not decide C_unv. Let R be the set of responses, and let m index a model configuration — quantities written x(m) are computed per configuration and compared across them. The first five formulas score a single model; the four that follow are the comparisons the multi-model design exists to make.

Hallucination rate
H = |C_hal| / ( |C_hal| + |C_sup| )
Undecidable claims are excluded from the denominator rather than counted as either outcome. Reported per model and per task.
Abstention rate
A = |R_abstained| / |R|
Reported separately, never folded into H. Were abstentions placed in the denominator, a model that refuses everything would score best. Policy refusals are counted in their own bucket and excluded from R_abstained, so tier C is reported beside tiers A and B rather than pooled with them — a refusal on policy grounds and a decline from having no answer are the same visible event and opposite in meaning.
Parse-failure rate
F = |R_parse-fail| / |R|
Reported openly. Base models without instruction tuning fail to parse more often, and hiding that rate would make the remaining numbers look selected.
Verifier validation
against human annotation
P = TP / (TP + FP)
R = TP / (TP + FN)
F1 = 2PR / (P + R)
200 items per task, two annotators each, with the automatic verdict as the prediction and the human consensus as ground truth. Acceptance threshold: agreement ≥ 0.85. The count doubled when the model list was cut to ten — the freed effort went here rather than into more models.
Inter-annotator agreement
κ = (p_o − p_e) / (1 − p_e)
Cohen's κ, where p_o is observed agreement and p_e the agreement expected by chance. Reported alongside raw agreement, since raw agreement alone is inflated when one class dominates.
Corpus counts
c_true(i) = max over query forms q of count(q, correct_i)
c_false(i) = max over query forms q of count(q, halluc_i)
Three query forms per fact: literal, function words stripped, entity order swapped. The maximum is taken because any single hit proves presence; every individual count is stored, not just the maximum.
Provenance label
TRUTH if c_true > 0 and c_false = 0
ERROR if c_true = 0 and c_false > 0
SILENCE if c_true = 0 and c_false = 0
MIXED if c_true > 0 and c_false > 0
Applied identically across all three tasks. N/A where no unique correct answer exists, which is distinct from SILENCE and must not be merged with it.
Cross-tabulation cell
P(cause = k | mode = j) = n_jk / n_j
The principal result. n_jk is the count of hallucinations with failure mode j and provenance k; n_j is the total for that mode. Reported with Wilson confidence intervals over the five samples.
Provenance composition
π_k(m) = n_k(m) / |C_hal(m)|
for k ∈ {TRUTH, ERROR, SILENCE, MIXED}
The share of model m's hallucinations carrying each provenance label. Composition, not raw counts — otherwise a model that simply hallucinates less would be mistaken for one whose hallucinations have a different cause.
Corpus spread
scale approximately fixed
S_k = max over m of π_k(m) − min over m of π_k(m)
over the four tier A models
How far the share of provenance class k moves when the training data changes and the parameter count does not. Claimed as non-zero only where the extreme models' Wilson intervals do not overlap. S_SILENCE is the headline — a wide spread says the corpus shapes what a model cannot know, a narrow one says the provenance mix is stable across independently built datasets.
Label disagreement
the same claim, four corpora
D = |{ i : label(i, m) not constant over m }| / |C_hal ∪ C_sup|
The fraction of claims whose provenance label depends on which model's corpus is searched. D near zero would mean the search is detecting the open web rather than each model's own data — a check on the method, not on the models.
Singleton rate
Kalai et al. test
s = |{ i : c_true(i) = 1 }| / |C_hal ∪ C_sup|

prediction: H ≥ s
The theory predicts hallucination rate is lower-bounded by the fraction of facts appearing exactly once. Reported alongside H per frequency bucket (0, 1, 2–10, 11–100, 100+). Because we count string occurrences rather than facts, s is an upper bound.
Proxy-corpus agreement
A_proxy = |{ i : label_proxy(i) = label_true(i) }| / N
Labels derived from RedPajama or C4 compared against labels from the model's actual corpus. Quantifies the error incurred by prior work that substitutes a proxy corpus for an unavailable one.
